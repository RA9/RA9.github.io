The Choices of Our Subconscious

A quiet taxi ride beside two grieving strangers, and a reflection on the small, repeated choices that slowly become who we are.

A quiet ride

Too many exciting things happened yesterday, but somehow, they were overshadowed by an experience that left a sadness in my heart.

I was sitting in a taxi when I noticed two passengers sitting at the back. They were crying softly. At first, I tried not to pay much attention, but their grief was impossible to ignore. Every time one of their phones rang, the conversation seemed to confirm the same painful news: they had lost their son to the cold hands of death.

The atmosphere inside the taxi changed almost immediately.

The music that had been playing in the background suddenly felt inappropriate. What had sounded like ordinary music a few minutes earlier now felt almost disturbing. The driver eventually turned off the radio. Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say.

I found myself fighting back tears too.

What remains

There is something about witnessing another person’s grief that forces you to confront your own mortality. You begin to think about life differently. You begin to wonder about the things we spend so much time pursuing, the arguments we hold onto, the people we hurt, the people we love but fail to appreciate, and the dreams we keep postponing as though tomorrow is guaranteed.

And my reflection that day was simple:

When death comes, our choices no longer matter to us. But the choices we made while alive may become the only thing people remember about us.

Whether he treated people well. Whether he was kind. Whether he fought through difficult seasons. Whether he was a good friend, a loving son, a responsible father, or simply someone who tried to make the lives of others a little easier.

All of it suddenly becomes part of a story that can no longer be rewritten.

Tomorrow is an assumption

The struggles he was fighting yesterday no longer have the same meaning. The things he was worried about, the bills he needed to pay, the plans he had for next month, the places he wanted to visit, the things he hoped to accomplish, the conversations he thought he had more time to have, all of them were suddenly interrupted.

Death has a strange way of reminding us that tomorrow is an assumption.

We live as though life is a contract that guarantees us another day. We make plans, postpone apologies, delay kindness, suppress affection, hold grudges, and convince ourselves that there will always be another opportunity.

Sometimes there will be.

Sometimes there won’t.

The choices of our subconscious

Perhaps this is where the choices of our subconscious become important.

We like to believe that we consciously choose everything about our lives. But much of what we do is shaped by patterns we have built over time. The way we respond to people. The way we speak when we are angry. The people we choose to forgive. The things we choose to ignore. The way we treat someone who cannot offer us anything in return.

These choices become so familiar that eventually we stop thinking about them.

They become instinctive.

And perhaps that is what the subconscious really reveals about us. Not necessarily what we say we value, but what we repeatedly choose when nobody is asking us to choose.

We may say we value family, but do we make time for them?

We may say we love our friends, but do we show up when they need us?

We may say we believe in kindness, but how do we treat people who cannot benefit us?

We may say life is precious, but how much of our lives do we spend consumed by things that will mean very little when our time eventually comes?

These questions are uncomfortable because they force us to look beyond our intentions and examine our patterns.

The small choices

Life is not only shaped by the big decisions we make. It is also shaped by the small choices we make repeatedly until they become part of our character.

A kind word.

A phone call.

An apology.

A little patience.

A decision not to retaliate.

A decision to help someone without expecting recognition.

A decision to tell someone you love them while you still have the opportunity.

These things may appear insignificant in the moment, but life is often remembered through insignificant moments.

Gentler with one another

Yesterday, two strangers reminded me of that without saying a word.

Their grief reminded me that behind every person we meet is a life we know very little about. Someone is fighting battles we may never see. Someone is carrying a burden behind their smile. Someone is waiting for a phone call. Someone is hoping for good news. Someone may be experiencing their last ordinary day without knowing it.

And that should make us gentler with one another.

Not because we should live in fear of death, but because we should live with an awareness of the value of life.

What the story will say

We cannot control when our story ends.

We cannot guarantee that every dream will be completed.

We cannot determine how long we will have with the people we love.

But while we are here, we can still choose what kind of people we want to be.

We can choose kindness over cruelty.

Forgiveness over resentment.

Presence over indifference.

Love over pride.

And purpose over simply existing.

Perhaps that is the real lesson death quietly teaches the living.

You may not get to choose how your story ends, but every day you are alive, you are choosing what the story will say about you.

And someday, when we are no longer here to explain ourselves, defend ourselves, or rewrite our decisions, those choices may be all that remains.

So perhaps we should pay closer attention to the choices we make when we think nobody is watching.

Because those choices are slowly becoming us.
