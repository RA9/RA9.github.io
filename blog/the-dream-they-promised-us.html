The Dream They Promised Us

We were handed one script: study hard, earn the degree, get the job, and the life you imagined will follow. For most of us, reality told a very different story.

The vision

In my vision of the future, I owned a collection of cars, held the keys to quiet mansions, took care of my family, and opened doors for everyone in my network. In my most generous moments, I went out of my way to make the world better: building schools, providing housing for the underprivileged, and offering a seat at the table to those who had long been excluded.

This was the dream: to be wealthy, to live with purpose, to accomplish something meaningful, and to become someone your family could point to with pride.

The script we were given

It was the exact script preached to us through more than thirteen years of basic schooling, then reinforced through universities, professional institutes, certifications, internships, and countless lecture halls. Study hard. Get good grades. Earn a degree. Secure a good job. Work hard. And eventually, the life you imagined will be yours.

At least, that is what we were conditioned to believe. But for most of us, reality told a very different story.

The glorified waiting room

For many, higher education never became the bridge to the promised land; it became a glorified waiting room. We graduated only to find a world that was not waiting for us with open arms. There were no guaranteed jobs, no automatic path to financial stability, and no comfortable lives laid out at the finish line. Instead, we were met with stacks of applications, silent rejections, unpaid internships, temporary contracts, underemployment, and the painful realization that a degree guarantees very little in a modern economy.

We spent years preparing to become employees, but almost no one taught us what to do when there were no jobs. School trained us to pass examinations, yet left us unequipped to generate real value. It conditioned us to follow instructions rather than identify problems worth solving. It forced us to compete for scarce opportunities instead of showing us how to create new ones.

The real deception

Perhaps the greatest deception was not that the dream was impossible, but that there was only one valid path to reach it.

Somewhere along the way, we confused education with success. We confused employment with wealth. We confused titles with impact, and busyness with genuine progress.

There is nothing wrong with wanting financial freedom, comfortable homes, or the ability to give your family security. Wanting more is human. The problem arises when we blindly inherit someone else's definition of success without ever pausing to ask what success actually means to us.

Redefining the dream

Maybe the dream isn't a mansion at all. Maybe it is having the freedom to spend unhurried time with your children. Maybe it is building a venture that puts fifty people to work, becoming the standard of excellence in your field, or building technology that solves an overlooked problem. Maybe it is earning enough to care for your aging parents without calculating every cent, or simply waking up every morning knowing your work carries weight.

The dream they promised us was a rigid destination. But the real dream is having the agency to design the journey ourselves. That requires an insight classrooms rarely provide: we have to stop waiting for permission to build our own lives.

Defining enough

The world does not owe us the dream. Our degrees will not deliver it, and neither will the system. At some point, the responsibility shifts, and we have to ask ourselves hard questions: What can I create? What problem can I solve? What value can I bring? What kind of life do I actually want to live?

Because ultimately, the dream was never about having everything. It was always about having the courage, knowledge, and freedom to define what enough means for ourselves.
